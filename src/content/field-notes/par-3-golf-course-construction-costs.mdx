## Why Short Courses Finally Pencil Out

Par 3 builds moved from novelty to main event programming sometime in the last five years, and the math is the reason. National Golf Foundation data puts short course openings at roughly 42 percent of all U.S. course openings from 2021 through 2025. The World Handicap System dropped its minimum length to 1,500 yards in 2024, which made properly designed par 3 layouts scorable and suddenly worth rating. Clubs that used to treat a par 3 nine as a kids' loop started looking at it as a second revenue center.

Operators figured out that a compact short course pulls dollars per square foot that a back nine cannot touch. One analysis making the rounds pegs a par 3 course at roughly 300 percent more revenue per yard than a standard 18, on about a third of the water. That kind of ratio moves boardrooms.

## What Par 3 Construction Actually Costs

Full length 18-hole builds in 2025 run five to fifteen million dollars for a standard facility, with premium builds pushing past twenty five. Per hole costs sit in a band from roughly 110,000 to 390,000 dollars, before any water features or elaborate bunkering.

Par 3 holes sit toward the low end of that band on paper, but the reality is messier. A par 3 green complex is usually as expensive as its full length cousin. The green itself is built to USGA specification the same way, the bunker construction is the same, and the tee complex is often more elaborate because you want four or five teeing options in a smaller footprint. The savings do not live in the greens. They live in what you do not have to build between them.

On a recent Carolinas Piedmont build with twelve holes averaging 135 yards, total hard construction came in at roughly 2.8 million dollars. The earthwork line was about 480,000 dollars against an 18-hole comparable that would have been north of two million. Irrigation dropped from a 1.6 million dollar install to around 520,000 because coverage area collapsed. Greens and bunkers were basically flat against an 18-hole build at about 1.3 million combined.

## Routing Short, Not Just Short Routing

The mistake on first generation par 3 courses was treating them as miniature 18s. Tee, fairway, green, repeat. That routing wastes the whole advantage of a short course.

Good short courses route around green complexes first. The greens are the product. You pick five to seven interesting pieces of ground with natural contour, build green sites that can host four or five different shot types depending on pin and tee selection, then connect them with the shortest walking transitions you can manage. On hilly ground you let elevation change dictate whether a hole plays 90 yards downhill or 190 yards across a saddle. On flat ground you push earth hard at the green sites and leave the tee corridors almost alone.

Walkability matters more than on a standard course. If a guest can play twelve par 3s in ninety minutes with a beer and a wedge, they come back. If the walk between 4 green and 5 tee is three hundred yards across a cart path, they do not. Sequencing the routing to keep green to tee transitions under thirty seconds of walk is the single design decision that makes or breaks repeat play.

## Earthwork Is Where the Savings Really Come From

Fairway earthwork on a par 3 build is close to zero if the routing is good. You are grading landing zones for a tee shot, not shaping 300 yard fairways with strategic contour. Cut and fill balance gets easier because total cubic yards drop by 60 to 75 percent against a full 18 on comparable terrain.

Subcontractor scheduling changes too. On an 18-hole mountain build in the Carolinas I sequenced through eighteen months of earthwork before a single irrigation trench opened. On a par 3 build of similar terrain, earthwork wrapped in four to six months, and irrigation and shaping overlapped in a way that would be chaos on a full course. Equipment on site dropped from four or five dozers and two or three excavators to two of each. That crew reduction is real money.

Weather windows matter more because the schedule is compressed. A Minnesota par 3 build has to hit its pad grades before first frost or the whole timeline slips into the following season. You cannot make up eight weeks of freeze-thaw on a short course the way you can absorb it across an 18-hole project that has plenty of non-frozen work left to do.

## Turf Selection and Grow-In on Compressed Schedules

Short courses grow in faster, and that is not incidental to the financial model. Bermuda sprigging on warm season sites can get you to first play in ten to twelve weeks if irrigation hits right and the weather cooperates. Bent greens in the transition zone take longer and cost more to maintain. On a short course the math has tilted hard toward ultradwarf bermuda greens for everything from eastern North Carolina south. Reported annual savings from dropping overseeding run 75,000 to 300,000 dollars depending on acreage, and pesticide savings on bermuda conversions run another 20,000 to 75,000. On a par 3 operation with thinner margin, those numbers decide the grass.

In cold climate builds, the play changes. I have worked sites in the upper Midwest where the only realistic grow-in window is May through September, and a par 3 going to bentgrass tees, fairways, and greens is the default because the heat and humidity that punishes bentgrass simply is not there. Budget the grow-in at seventy to ninety days of intensive hand watering on greens regardless of the region. That labor does not get cheaper because the course is shorter.

## How Operators Pencil the Pro Forma

The financial case for a par 3 gets made on utilization and dwell. Green fees run 25 to 60 percent of the flagship 18, but rounds per acre are three to five times higher. Add food and beverage revenue from a short course routed past the clubhouse terrace, music programming, lighting for after dark play, and you get a facility that does not compete with the championship course but feeds it.

Capital deployment staggers well too. A developer can phase a par 3 as a first mover on a larger master plan, get cash flow coming in twelve to eighteen months sooner, and use the operating data to support the main course pro forma. Lenders like that a lot more than a single twelve million dollar line on a master schedule that does not produce revenue for two and a half years.

**The Read.** Par 3 construction is going to keep eating share of new golf development because the spreadsheet works, not because the category is trendy. Operators who want to play in this lane should budget for real greens, plan the routing around walking experience rather than traditional hole sequence, and pick turf on operating economics rather than nostalgia. The ground moves less on a par 3 build, which means design intent shows up faster, and so do the mistakes.
