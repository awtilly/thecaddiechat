The deal Henrico County announced on April 17, 2026, is the kind of transaction that sounds simple in a press release and looks substantially more complicated when you try to price it.

McConnell Golf, a North Carolina based operator of private clubs across the Carolinas and Virginia, is acquiring The Crossings Golf Club, a 268 acre property in Glen Allen that has been sitting closed for years. Nominal purchase price: one dollar. That is not a typo. The Henrico Economic Development Authority is handing the land over for a dollar, and McConnell is agreeing to spend roughly 6 to 7 million dollars rebuilding the course and clubhouse. The EDA and the Henrico Sports & Entertainment Authority are kicking in another 2 million or so for clubhouse and facility upgrades. Total public and private capital in motion: about 9 million dollars on a parcel that once traded for several times that figure.

## How the capital stack actually works

A dollar sale makes the headline, but the real structure here is a land contribution in exchange for a contractual rebuild. Henrico acquired the course itself in 2024 after the private ownership unraveled and the site deteriorated. Rather than stand up a public operation on the tax base, the county is treating the land as equity. McConnell brings the operating expertise, the construction capital, and the obligation to actually reopen the place as a daily fee public course by late 2027. If McConnell walks, the county is presumably protected by clawback provisions, though none of the reporting I have seen spells out exactly what happens if the project slips or the capital plan changes.

For a deal at this scale, roughly 34,000 dollars per acre of combined public and private investment, the economics only work if the back end materializes. The county is not really buying a golf course. It is buying a tournament venue, with sports tourism events like a Korn Ferry Tour stop, possibly an LPGA date, and the economic development halo that comes with nationally televised golf in Glen Allen. That is the bet. Whether it cashes depends on factors that have very little to do with what any architect eventually draws.

## The construction window is tight

Let us talk about the schedule. The course has been closed for multiple seasons. Irrigation has sat idle. Greens have either gone to native or been overtaken by whatever the prevailing seed bank wants to do with a warm Virginia summer. Bunker sand has turned into soil. The clubhouse, last refreshed at scale years before the closure, needs a comprehensive rebuild to host tournament infrastructure, media, and hospitality. McConnell is targeting a fall 2027 reopening.

Backing into that date: finalize approvals through the summer of 2026, mobilize by early fall, push earthwork and rough shaping through spring 2027, then grow in through the summer. In central Virginia, that is a viable sequence if the permits cooperate and nothing material shifts on site. The honest question is whether a full 18 hole rebuild to 7,200 plus yards, with tournament specification drainage and greens complexes, can actually be delivered in roughly 18 months of construction and a single grow in window. It is doable. It is not comfortable. My guess is the fall 2027 target slips into winter or early 2028 if there is one wet spring, and nobody should be surprised.

## The design question that has not been answered

The story being told publicly is that McConnell handles the design work with its in-house team. That matters. McConnell is an operator with a growing club portfolio, not a design firm with a public portfolio in the sense that Coore and Crenshaw or Gil Hanse are. The in-house approach keeps costs lower and aligns the design tightly with how the company intends to operate the asset, but it also means the course will not carry the marketing lift that a named architect provides. You can sell a Tom Doak renovation to tournament committees in a way you cannot sell an in-house refresh, even if the ground game is equally good.

Earlier iterations of the Henrico plan, when the county was partnering with Pros Inc., name checked Lester George, a Virginia architect with a serious restoration track record and one of the best sets of eyes on mid Atlantic parkland golf. Whether George or someone with a comparable profile ends up consulting on the final design is the piece I would watch. A public course with PGA Tour Champions or Korn Ferry ambition needs a routing that holds up to scoring pressure, and the spread between an in-house rebuild and a named architect rebuild is probably the difference between a pleasant daily fee and a genuinely distinctive tournament venue.

## Where this goes next

The tell is the next 90 days. If McConnell names a consulting architect with actual tournament design pedigree, the county's bet looks defensible. If the design team stays entirely in-house and the scope gets quietly trimmed, the Korn Ferry conversation fades. My guess is the project opens, probably three to six months later than advertised, hosts a regional amateur within a year of opening, and spends a decade building the case for a Tour event that may or may not ever arrive. A dollar buys a lot of optionality. It does not guarantee the optionality gets exercised.
